# The Boron Letters: structure and list

Assume the book. Halbert's letters from prison to his son are not summarized
here. What follows is the part of the method that decides where effort goes,
the structural skeleton, and an honest account of which Halbert moves are dead
or counterproductive on modern surfaces.

## What this skill owns

`copy-strategist` calls it as step 3, the structure layer: headline and A-pile
first, then AIDA, then fascinations. It gets added to a stack when a headline
or subject line carries the piece, when the format is email or long-form, or
when list and audience quality is the real question.

If you keep your own tested opener patterns (hooks that have actually worked
for you, with results), those outrank this file for openers. Point the skill
at them.

## The rule that reorders the work: 40/40/20

Forty percent of the result is the list, forty percent is the offer, and twenty
percent is the copy. Halbert's real argument is that writers spend their time
in inverse proportion to what matters.

For content rather than mail: audience and topic fit is the first forty, the
idea itself is the second forty, and the wording is the twenty. When a piece
underperforms, interrogate in that order. A brilliant sentence attached to the
wrong topic for the wrong reader is the most common failure, and no rewrite
reaches it. Ask which stage failed rather than which line was weak.

## The starving crowd

Given a choice of advantages in selling a hamburger, take the starving crowd.
Market before message, always. In practice this means the audience question
gets answered before the copy question, and a piece aimed at nobody in
particular cannot be rescued at the sentence level.

## A-pile and B-pile

Mail gets sorted into what opens now and what gets binned. The test that
survives the move to screens: by the end of line 2, the target reader has
located themselves in the piece.

## AIDA

The structural skeleton, kept compact because the model knows it and the value
is in the ordering. Attention gets the right reader, not the most readers.
Interest keeps them past the first block. Desire makes them want the outcome,
which is where `cashvertising` takes over. Action tells them exactly what to
do, which is where `dan-kennedy-copy` takes over.

Use it as a skeleton for long-form and email. It is a poor fit for short-form
video, where a cause-and-effect beat structure ("but... therefore...") works
better.

## What survives

- **The A-pile locate-yourself test.**
- **Market before message.** The 40/40/20 ordering is the most useful thing in
  the book for modern content work.
- **Specificity.** Halbert and Whitman agree here.
- **Read aloud, sleep on it, then cut.** Read the draft out loud and rewrite
  wherever the tongue stumbles.

## What is dead, and what collides

**Dead for most modern work,** and not worth adapting:

- Physical mail tactics. Grabbers, lumpy envelopes, and stamp psychology stay
  relevant only for a campaign that actually mails things.
- The Halbert sales-letter template, unless you are writing a sales letter.
- Hand-copying great ads. It is a learning exercise for a copywriter, never a
  drafting instruction, and it should never appear in a handoff as a step.

**Collides with plain modern voice:**

- **Fascination craft as the book teaches it.** Halbert's bullets run on
  dashes, curiosity gaps ("hint: it has nothing to do with meditation", "page
  12"), and unearned promises ("while DOUBLING my output"). To a reader who
  knows you, the curiosity gap reads as a tactic, and the promise needs a
  receipt first. The underlying mechanic survives: a specific claim plus a
  reason to keep reading. Write the bullet as a full clause that does work.
- **Bullet stacks.** Three or more consecutive bullets of one or two words
  each reads as slides pasted into prose. Expand each into a clause or fold
  the items into a sentence.
- **Long copy wins.** True for a sales letter and false on a feed, where a
  mobile post shows roughly 140 characters above the fold and short-form video
  runs 30 to 90 seconds. Length is set by the format, and
  `copywriting-awareness` sets it by stage.

## The drafting process, which does transfer

Gather the research into one place, write everything without editing, then read
it aloud and fix every stumble. Sleep on it when the timing allows. Cut the
unnecessary words, delete what bores you, and add proof wherever a claim is
thin. That last step is where `[PROOF NEEDED]` markers get resolved against a
real source rather than filled with a plausible number.

## Handoff

Return the structural choice (which skeleton, which opener shape, where the
proof sits) rather than a finished draft when `copy-strategist` is composing.

If you have a voice guide or copy checker, anything drafted here, including
suggested headlines and bullets, goes through it before it is handed back.

## Boundaries

It does not verify claims, set the desire, or own the offer. It cannot fix a
piece aimed at the wrong audience, which is the whole point of the 40/40/20
ordering, and it makes no promise about commercial results.
