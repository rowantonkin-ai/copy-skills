# Kennedy: the offer and response layer

Assume the material. The ten rules are not re-taught here. This skill has the
narrowest live scope of the five, because Kennedy assumes something to sell
and a lot of brand and content work is deliberately not selling anything.

## Where it actually applies

**Your live offers.** The sales page, booking page, or consult call at the
bottom of your funnel is where the full Kennedy apparatus belongs: the offer,
the objection bank, the intake, and a plainly stated next step.

**A rationed CTA slot.** If your content plan limits commercial asks (for
example, one per week across all channels), the piece that spends that slot
is the Kennedy one and the rest are not.

**Client work.** An engagement for another business uses Kennedy unmodified,
and often should, because the client usually does have something to sell.

Everywhere else, the answer to "what is the offer" may be that there is not
one, and reaching for this skill will make the piece worse.

## The ten rules, and which survive in brand-building work

Six carry over cleanly. There is always an offer where an offer exists, the
instructions are unmistakable, the copy sells rather than informs, follow-up
happens, testing beats guessing, and discipline means staying with what works.

Four need handling.

**Rule 5, no brand waste, would wreck a top-of-funnel strategy if applied
literally.** Kennedy holds that advertising which cannot be traced to a
response is money burned. Social content is often built the other way on
purpose: it exists to make the problem felt and prove the author is real, and
it is measured on audience growth rather than on response. Do not let this
rule argue the top of the funnel out of existence. It applies from the
newsletter or email list down, where a response is the actual job.

**Rule 2, urgency, often does not transfer.** Countdown timers, seat counts,
and "price goes up Monday" read as pressure where you are building a
relationship. Kennedy's sloth test asks whether the urgency forces movement
now, and for most relationship content the honest answer is that nothing
should. Real constraints stated plainly are fine at the bottom of the funnel,
because they are true.

**Rule 4, tracking, may be aspirational.** Organic social analytics are often
thin, and numbers may arrive by manual export. Build whatever regular score
read you can, speak in odds rather than certainties, and do not promise
attribution the instruments cannot deliver.

**Rule 8, mail-order format, is dead** unless the campaign actually mails
things.

## Threshold resistance, rebuilt

Kennedy's structure pairs a low-threshold offer with a high-threshold one. The
low-threshold half in its classic form is a gated free report. If your brand
avoids gating, the replacement is to make the low-threshold offer the artifact
itself, given away in the channel's native format with no form in front of it.

The pair becomes a working piece of what you know handed over for free, plus
the paid offer for the reader who is ready. The ladder still functions. The
gate comes out of it.

## What survives without qualification

Write to the psyche rather than to the product. Make the next step
unmistakable, and make it one step rather than three. Reverse the risk where
risk is the real objection. Ask for the response you actually want instead of
hoping the reader infers it.

## Handoff

State the offer, the single next step, and whether the piece is spending a
rationed commercial CTA. When the brief has no offer, say so plainly rather
than inventing one, and hand back to `copy-strategist` for a different close.

If you have a voice guide or copy checker, anything drafted here, including
suggested CTAs, goes through it before it is handed back.

## Boundaries

It cannot invent an offer where none exists, manufacture urgency that is not
real, or deliver attribution the platforms do not expose. Pricing, guarantees,
and what gets promised commercially are the human's calls.
