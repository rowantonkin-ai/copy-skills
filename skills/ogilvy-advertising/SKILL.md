# Ogilvy: the idea and brand layer

Assume the books. Ogilvy's career and rules are not retold here. What follows
is the part of his method the rest of the stack is missing: deciding what the
piece says before anyone writes it, finding the fact that makes it believable,
and keeping every piece consistent with the brand.

The other five skills are direct response. They get a reader to act now, and
they all assume the core message is already settled. Ogilvy started earlier,
with the product, and he cared about the brand the tenth ad would build as
much as the reply the first one would get.

## What this skill owns

1. **Step 0, the idea.** Positioning, the Big Idea, and one hard product fact.
   It runs before `copywriting-awareness`, because the stage tells you where
   to enter and this tells you what you are there to say.
2. **The brand-to-response dial.** Where the piece sits between building the
   brand and getting a response, which tells `copy-strategist` how much weight
   to give Ogilvy versus Kennedy and Halbert.
3. **Image, headline and caption,** for any format with a picture.
4. **The final brand check,** after the draft and any voice check.

Headline craft belongs to `boron-letters` and desire to `cashvertising`. This
skill adds two headline points below and otherwise defers.

## Who this is for

Assume the user is good at their product and not at copy. Do not ask them for
a "positioning statement" or a "Big Idea". Ask the plain questions below, do
the translation yourself, propose options, and explain the recommendation in
one ordinary sentence. Name the framework only if they ask.

## Step 0a: the plain-questions interview

Ask only what the brief and project files do not already answer, at most five
at a time.

1. What does it do, in one sentence, as you would say it to a friend?
2. Who is it for? Picture one real customer. What were they doing right before
   they needed this?
3. What would they use instead if you did not exist? (Including "nothing" or
   "a spreadsheet".)
4. What is one thing about how it is made, built, or delivered that you are
   proud of and have never bothered to advertise?
5. What do customers say when they like it? Use their words if you have them.

If they cannot answer one, move on and infer, and say what you inferred.

## Step 0b: dig out the fact

Ogilvy's most famous car headline came from a line buried in an engineering
report about how quiet the car was. The best copy usually starts from a
specific, checkable fact about the product, not from an adjective.

Look for facts in these places:

- **How it is made.** Materials, steps, tests, the thing the team does that
  competitors skip.
- **Numbers.** Time saved, how many, how long, how often, how small. Real ones.
- **Proof of care.** Guarantees, inspections, what happens when it goes wrong.
- **The boring detail.** The thing insiders take for granted. It is often the
  most persuasive fact because outsiders have never heard it.
- **Customer outcomes.** What changed for a real customer, in their words.

A fact that every competitor could also claim, but none of them says, still
works. Whoever says it first and plainly tends to own it.

Never invent a fact. If none is available, mark `[FACT NEEDED: ...]` with the
question that would find it, and draft the idea without it.

## Step 0c: positioning and the Big Idea

**Positioning** is what the product does and who it is for. Write three
candidates in this shape and recommend one:

> For [specific reader], [product] is the [what it is] that [does what],
> because [the fact].

**The Big Idea** is the single thought the piece, and ideally a whole run of
pieces, is built around. Generate three and test each:

- **Is it tied to the product?** Cleverness that could sell anything sells
  nothing. The idea should fall apart if you swap the product out.
- **Could a competitor run it?** If yes, it is a category claim, not an idea.
- **Would the reader notice it?** An idea that reads as expected will be
  skipped.
- **Could it run for a year?** One-off jokes wear out. An idea can take many
  executions.
- **Does it sell?** Ogilvy's filter: work that does not sell is not creative,
  however clever. When two options tie, pick the one that makes the product's
  value clearer.

Hand back the recommended positioning, the recommended idea, the fact under
it, and one sentence on why, written for someone who does not know the
terms.

## The brand-to-response dial

Ask one question: is this piece mainly meant to make people remember and
trust the brand, or to make them do something now?

- **Brand end** (social posts, about pages, brand ads, top of funnel): weight
  this skill and `copywriting-awareness`. Consistency, tone, and the idea
  matter more than the CTA. A soft next step is fine.
- **Middle** (newsletters, most landing pages): balance. Keep the idea, add a
  clear single action from `dan-kennedy-copy`.
- **Response end** (offer emails, sales pages, retargeting): weight
  `dan-kennedy-copy` and `boron-letters`. This skill only runs the final brand
  check so the hard sell still sounds like you.

Ogilvy's view was that every piece either adds to or spends the brand's
reputation. Response copy is allowed to be direct. It is not allowed to sound
like a different company.

## Two headline points, then defer

`boron-letters` owns headline structure. Ogilvy adds two things beginners get
wrong most:

- **Most people read only the headline.** If the benefit is not in it, most
  readers never meet the benefit.
- **Facts beat adjectives.** "Fast", "powerful" and "innovative" say nothing.
  The fact from Step 0b says something. Put it in the headline when it fits.

## Image, headline and caption

For any format with a picture (ads, landing pages, carousels, thumbnails):

- **The image and headline tell the same story.** If the picture needs the
  copy to explain it, or the copy needs the picture to make sense, fix one.
- **Story appeal.** An image with something slightly unexpected in it makes
  people look twice. Ogilvy's shirt ad put an eye patch on the model. Pick a
  detail that fits the brand; do not bolt on a gimmick.
- **Caption every image that carries meaning.** People read captions more than
  body copy. Use the caption to carry the fact.
- **Show the product in use,** or the result of using it, over a stock photo of
  a smiling person.
- **Make it look like something worth reading.** Ads laid out like editorial
  content were read more than ads that looked like ads. The modern version: a
  post that looks like a useful note beats one that looks like a banner.

## The final brand check

Run after the draft and any voice or style check. Four questions:

1. Does this sound like the same brand as the last ten pieces? If there are no
   past pieces, does it match the tone the user described?
2. Swap in a competitor's name. Does it still work? If yes, the idea or the
   fact is missing.
3. Is anything talking down to the reader? Ogilvy's rule was to treat the
   reader as intelligent. Plain is good; condescending is not.
4. Is there a fact where an adjective used to be?

Report the answers in the handoff. Fix what fails, or say what is open.

## What is dated, and what collides

- **Print layout rules.** Much of Ogilvy's layout research comes from
  magazines and newspapers from the 1960s to the 1980s. Keep the principles
  above; drop the exact type and column rules.
- **Brand name in the headline.** Sensible in print, redundant on social,
  where the account name already sits above every post.
- **Long copy.** He argued informative long copy outsells short. That still
  holds on a sales page and breaks on a feed. Format sets the length, and
  `copywriting-awareness` sets it by stage.
- **The era's phrasing.** Some of his lines about the reader are worded in a
  way that has aged badly. Keep the idea (respect the reader's intelligence)
  and do not quote the wording.

## Handoff

Return, in plain language:

- The recommended positioning and Big Idea, with the two runners-up.
- The product fact under the idea, or the `[FACT NEEDED]` question.
- Where the piece sits on the brand-to-response dial.
- For visual formats, the image and caption direction.
- At the end, the four brand-check answers.

If you have a voice guide or copy checker, anything drafted here goes through
it before it is handed back.

## Boundaries

It cannot verify that a fact is true, and it cannot invent one. It does not
replace customer research: if the user does not know who the customer is, say
so and suggest they talk to five customers before spending money on the copy.
Final brand, ethical and legal judgment stays with the human, and it makes no
promise about commercial results.
